The Weight of Shelter

A Novel of Collapse and Conscience

The first time Micah Carver asked Lena Patel if Gray Fever was something he should actually be worried about, she didn’t answer him right away.

That bothered him more than if she had simply said yes.

They were sitting in a booth near the front window of a small restaurant a few blocks from his plant, halfway through a lunch they had already rescheduled twice because Lena’s schedule had become increasingly unpredictable.

Outside, nothing looked particularly apocalyptic.

Cars rolled through the intersection. A delivery truck sat double-parked across the street. Two construction workers leaned against the tailgate of a pickup, eating sandwiches out of paper wrappers.

Someone had taped a handwritten sign to the restaurant door:

PLEASE DO NOT ENTER IF YOU ARE EXPERIENCING FEVER, COUGHING, OR SEVERE FATIGUE.

A month ago, Micah probably would have laughed at it.

Today, he had counted three similar signs before they even sat down.

“So?” he asked.

Lena tore open another packet of sweetener and poured it into her coffee.

“So what?”

“You know what.”

“I know a lot of things.”

Micah pointed at her with a fry. “That thing you’ve been avoiding answering for the last ten seconds.”

She stirred her coffee without looking up.

“There it is again.”

Lena glanced at him. “What?”

“The doctor face.”

“I don’t have a doctor face.”

“You absolutely have a doctor face.”

She leaned back slightly. “What does my doctor face look like?”

“Like you’re deciding how much information I’m emotionally qualified to receive.”

A small smile pulled at the corner of her mouth.

“Your emotional qualifications have always been questionable.”

“See? Deflection.”

“Micah.”

“Lena.”

She looked down at her coffee again.

His smile faded.

They had known each other long enough that silence carried information.

Micah picked up another fry, reconsidered, and dropped it onto his plate.

“A couple people at work have been sick,” he said.

Lena looked up. “People get sick.”

“Not like this.”

Her attention sharpened.

“One guy’s been out almost two weeks. Another came back Monday and looked like somebody had kicked the hell out of him. Barely made it through half the day.”

“Did he have a fever?”

“I don’t know.”

“Cough?”

“I don’t know.”

“Shortness of breath?”

Micah spread his hands. “I run inventory, Lena. I don’t perform physicals.”

“Then maybe don’t diagnose your coworkers over lunch.”

“I didn’t diagnose anybody.” He nodded toward her. “That’s why I’m asking the doctor sitting across from me.”

Lena exhaled through her nose.

Gray Fever had been on the news for several weeks, though nobody seemed capable of agreeing on exactly what it was.

At first, it had been a regional illness somewhere else.

Then several states.

Then suspected cases closer to home.

Officials kept repeating the same language. The situation was under investigation. Hospitals were prepared. Public-health agencies were monitoring developments. People should remain calm and take reasonable precautions.

The kind of language meant to reassure people without saying very much at all.

Small protests had started a few days earlier.

Nothing serious yet.

People with signs outside government buildings. Arguments about school restrictions and proposed quarantines. Videos online of angry people shouting at officials and officials trying not to shout back.

At work, most conversations about Gray Fever still ended with someone comparing it to the flu.

Micah had mostly done the same.

Until his coworkers started disappearing.

Lena folded her hands on the table.

“Yes,” she said.

Micah waited.

“Yes what?”

“Yes. I think you should be paying attention.”

“That’s different than worried.”

“It is.”

“Should I be worried?”

This time, the pause was shorter.

“I’m worried.”

The humor left him.

Micah studied her.

Lena had always been good at giving bad information calmly.

He knew that better than most people.

“So what aren’t they telling us?”

“I didn’t say anybody wasn’t telling you something.”

“You didn’t have to.”

Her expression tightened. “Don’t do that.”

“Do what?”

“Turn every hesitation into evidence of a conspiracy.”

“I didn’t say conspiracy.”

“You were about six seconds away.”

“Five.”

She didn’t smile.

That told him enough.

“Some of the guidance coming through hospitals has changed,” Lena said. “We’ve been told to increase isolation capability. Minimize unnecessary contact with respiratory cases. Review surge procedures.”

“How unusual is that?”

“By itself? Not enormously.”

“And together?”

Lena looked toward the front window.

A man walking past wore a surgical mask. Another man coming the opposite direction gave him a long look as they crossed paths.

“Enough that I’m paying attention.”

Micah finally ate the fry.

“So I’m not buying a bunker yet.”

“You couldn’t afford a bunker.”

“That’s reassuring.”

“You asked.”

He watched her for another moment.

There were questions Lena could answer that he chose not to ask.

That was part of why they were still friends.

A little over two years earlier, she had sat beside him during the worst week of his life.

She had known when to explain things.

More importantly, she had known when explanations would only make them worse.

Afterward, when almost everyone else had either tried too hard to help or gradually stopped knowing what to say, Lena had simply kept showing up.

Lunch sometimes.

Coffee.

A phone call.

Occasionally weeks between any of it.

Nothing romantic. Nothing complicated.

Just one of the few relationships in his life that had survived something neither of them discussed unless there was a reason.

His phone buzzed against the table.

Then Lena’s did.

Then one behind him.

A second later, half the restaurant seemed to vibrate.

Micah picked up his phone.

The notification filled the screen.

STATE HEALTH DEPARTMENT CONFIRMS FIRST GRAY FEVER FATALITIES

He read it twice.

“First?”

Lena already had her phone in her hand.

Her face changed.

Barely.

That was what frightened him.

Around them, conversations began to die.

Someone near the counter muttered, “Jesus.”

A woman told her husband to check his phone.

The television above the bar switched from a basketball discussion to a breaking-news banner.

Six confirmed deaths.

Then eight.

The anchor spoke too quickly, trying to keep up with whatever was appearing in front of him.

Micah looked across the table.

“You knew people were dying.”

Lena shook her head. “I knew there were patients deteriorating.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

She met his eyes.

“No. I didn’t know they were going to announce this today.”

Another alert appeared.

Then another.

Additional suspected fatalities.

Confirmed transmission in multiple counties.

An emergency press conference within the hour.

Micah turned toward the window.

The street still looked normal.

That somehow made it worse.

The delivery truck was still there.

The construction workers were still eating.

A woman stepped out of a shop carrying two bags and struggled briefly with the door.

Everything looked exactly the same.

Apparently it wasn’t.

“You need to go back to the hospital?” he asked.

“Probably.”

“Probably?”

Lena held up her phone. “It hasn’t stopped vibrating.”

“That’s generally considered a clue.”

She ignored him and started gathering her things.

Micah pulled out his wallet.

“I’ve got it.”

“We can split it.”

“You’re about to go deal with a mysterious disease. Let me buy you a sandwich.”

“You make it sound very heroic.”

“I saw you eat half my fries.”

“You weren’t eating them.”

“That isn’t legally relevant.”

The television volume increased.

A government official stood behind a podium.

The restaurant quieted again.

The words came in pieces.

Rapid spread.

Confirmed deaths.

Avoid unnecessary gatherings.

Individuals experiencing symptoms should isolate immediately.

Employers should prepare for additional public-health guidance.

Then came something different.

Residents were being advised to return home where possible and remain indoors pending further instructions.

Someone near the door swore.

A chair scraped hard across the floor.

Three people stood almost at once.

Micah looked at Lena.

“That’s new.”

“Yes.”

“How new?”

“Very.”

He dropped enough cash onto the table to cover both meals.

“Come on.”

Outside, the street had changed in less than five minutes.

It hadn’t collapsed.

Not yet.

But people were walking faster.

Cars had begun stacking at the intersection. A man stood beside his vehicle talking loudly into his phone. Across the street, the construction workers had thrown away their food and were loading equipment into the truck.

Micah and Lena headed toward the parking lot.

“You parked far?”

“Other side.”

“I’ll walk you.”

She glanced at him. “I am capable of crossing a parking lot.”

“I’ve heard.”

“Remarkable medical breakthrough.”

“Huge.”

Another alert sounded.

Micah pulled out his phone while they walked.

The advisory had been updated.

RETURN HOME. AVOID PHYSICAL CONTACT WITH INDIVIDUALS OUTSIDE YOUR HOUSEHOLD. DO NOT APPROACH PERSONS DISPLAYING GRAY FEVER SYMPTOMS.

He stopped.

Lena nearly walked into him.

“What?”

He turned the screen toward her.

She read it.

Around them, car doors slammed.

Someone shouted from across the parking lot.

A horn started blaring and didn’t stop.

Micah watched her face.

“How bad is this?”

This time, Lena answered immediately.

“I don’t know.”

He had known Lena Patel long enough to understand exactly how frightened he should be when a doctor said that.

His phone rang.

Marcus.

Micah looked at the name, then back at Lena before answering.

“Hey.”

Marcus skipped hello.

“You watching this shit?”

“Yeah.”

A crash sounded somewhere beyond the parking lot.

Micah and Lena both turned toward it.

Nothing they could see.

Yet.

“Where’s Lily?” Micah asked.

“At the shop with me.”

“And Ben?”

“Here.”

Micah closed his eyes for half a second.

Of course he was.

“Stay there.”

Marcus paused. “For what?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“That isn’t exactly inspiring confidence.”

“Give me five minutes.”

Micah ended the call.

Lena was staring at him.

“What?”

“You just told him to stay there.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He looked toward the road.

Traffic was backing up in both directions now.

Farther off, somewhere closer to downtown, people were shouting.

Micah had no plan.

That was the part nobody else would remember later.

He was scared.

He didn’t know whether they should go home, go to the hospital, find Marcus, call Jacob, or listen to the government and lock themselves inside the nearest building.

Every option felt wrong.

But Marcus had his daughter with him.

Ben was there too.

And Micah wasn’t leaving them in a mechanic shop while the city started coming apart.

“Because I’m going to get them.”

Lena stared at him for another second.

Then she reached into her purse and pulled out her keys.

“Then I’m driving.”

Micah frowned. “Why?”

“Because while you figure out what terrible plan you’re about to make, one of us should watch the road.”

Despite everything, he smiled.

“There she is.”

Lena pointed toward the car.

“Get in, Micah.”

He did.
